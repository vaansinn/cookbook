## Prep ahead
- Brunoise the onion, carrot and celery — a fine, even dice cooks into the sauce instead of staying chunky
- Rinse the red lentils
- Have the wine measured out before the pan is hot — deglazing waits for no one

## Ingredients
- 220|220 g dry spaghetti|pasta_dry
- 150|150 g red lentils, rinsed|lentils_red_dry
- 110|1 onion, fine brunoise|onion
- 60|1 carrot, fine brunoise|carrot
- 40|1 celery stalk, fine brunoise|celery
- 10|2 garlic cloves, minced|garlic
- 400|1 can chopped tomatoes (400 g)|tomatoes_canned
- 32|2 tbsp tomato paste|tomato_paste
- 50|50 ml dry red wine|red_wine
- 13.5|1 tbsp olive oil|olive_oil
- 14|1 tbsp butter, cold, cubed|butter
- 10|A few fresh basil leaves, torn|basil_fresh
- 300|Vegetable bouillon + ~300 ml water|vegetable_bouillon
- 20|Grated parmesan to serve|parmesan
- 2.3|A pinch of black pepper|black_pepper

## Instructions
1. Heat the oil in a heavy pot over medium heat. Add the brunoise and sweat slowly, 10-12 min, until soft and sweet with no colour — don't rush this, it's most of the sauce's depth.
2. Push the vegetables aside, add the tomato paste directly to the pot, and let it fry and darken for 1 min — this builds fond, not just flavours the paste.
3. Deglaze with the red wine, scraping up the browned bits from the bottom. Let it bubble down by half, 1-2 min.
4. Add the lentils, chopped tomatoes, bouillon and water. Simmer uncovered 25-30 min, stirring occasionally, until the lentils have broken down and the sauce coats a spoon.
5. Meanwhile, cook the spaghetti in well-salted water until just shy of al dente. Reserve a cup of pasta water.
6. Off the heat, whisk the cold butter cubes into the sauce a piece at a time (mantecare) — this is what makes it glossy rather than just wet. Fold in the pasta and a splash of pasta water, tossing until the sauce clings.
7. Finish with torn basil, black pepper, and parmesan.

## Notes
- Brunoise is a knife-skill worth practising here: uniform small dice means the vegetables melt into the sauce at the same rate instead of some going mushy while others stay raw.
- Deglazing (scraping the browned bits off the pot with liquid) is what turns a good sauce into a deep one — don't skip it even if you're out of wine; a splash of the bouillon works too.
- Mantecare (finishing off-heat with cold butter, swirled not stirred) is a classic Italian technique for a silky sauce that coats instead of pools.
- Equipment: one heavy pot (sauce) + one pot (pasta).
